# Candidate communication standard

> Shared decision rules for LinkedIn notes, application answers, and cover letters. Channel playbooks own their inputs, length, structure, and output format.

## Goal before evidence

Decide what the writing must accomplish before choosing a project, skill, or metric. Evidence earns space only when it helps this reader understand Shubh or take the intended next step.

An impressive fact is not automatically a useful fact. If a reader needs project history, a technical baseline, or another sentence of explanation before a number means anything, choose a clearer capability or omit the number.

## Decision sequence

Run these steps internally before drafting:

1. Identify the reader and the response the writing should earn.
2. Extract the reader's most relevant problem, responsibility, or area of work from the supplied profile or JD.
3. Generate two or three candidate angles from verified facts.
4. Reject angles that require hidden context, force an irrelevant metric, exaggerate overlap, repeat what the reader already knows, or could be reused unchanged for many readers.
5. Rank the remaining angles by relevance, standalone clarity, credibility, usefulness toward the desired response, and space cost.
6. Choose one primary angle. Add supporting evidence only when the channel or question requires it.
7. Draft for the channel.
8. Run the final rejection pass and rewrite until it passes.

Do not expose this reasoning unless the channel playbook requests a short decision summary. Return one final candidate-facing version, not a menu of alternatives.

## Evidence ranking

Prefer evidence in this order:

1. Directly relevant work with an outcome the reader can understand.
2. A directly relevant implementation decision or responsibility.
3. An adjacent capability described honestly.
4. A project name or metric only when it is useful without hidden context.

Judge every candidate fact on five dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| Relevance | Does this help answer this reader's likely question? |
| Standalone clarity | Will it make sense without knowing the project already? |
| Credibility | Is the claim verified and framed at the correct maturity level? |
| Usefulness | Does it help earn the intended response? |
| Space cost | Is it worth the words or characters it consumes? |

Choose the strongest complete explanation, not the largest number or most sophisticated stack.

### Metric rule

Omit the metric when the reader needs domain knowledge, a technical baseline, or project background to judge it. Replace it with the capability or problem solved when that is clearer.

Use a metric only when all are true:

- its authoritative fact file permits it;
- a reader with no project context understands why it matters;
- it strengthens the chosen angle;
- the channel has enough space to explain it honestly.

## Clarification threshold

Ask only when missing information would materially change the output and no safe default exists.

Do not ask merely because:

- several facts could work; rank them and choose;
- a JD spans multiple lanes; select the dominant responsibility;
- a company sounds ordinary; focus on the work without inventing enthusiasm;
- a metric needs explanation; omit the metric;
- LinkedIn intent is unstated; use the channel's default reply-oriented networking goal.

Ask one focused question when:

- personalization would otherwise require inventing a detail;
- the difference between applied and not applied changes the requested outreach;
- an answer depends on Shubh's unstored personal motivation or preference;
- salary, relocation, legal history, work authorization, or another consequential fact is not recorded authoritatively.

When clarification is required, state what is missing and ask only for that information. Do not draft around the gap.

## Truth boundary

Candidate claims come only from:

- `resume-system/facts/work-experience.md`;
- current selectable files under `resume-system/facts/projects/`;
- `resume-system/governance/FACT_RULES.md`.

Use `career-tools/linkedin/candidate-profile.md` only as a routing aid. Confirm any selected fact or metric in its authoritative source.

Never use `career-stories/_drafts/`, old prompt examples, legacy resumes, or plausible inference as evidence. Never combine unrelated facts into a fictional story.

Preserve claim maturity:

- Designed means designed.
- Built means working code is confirmed.
- Deployed or production means a live environment is confirmed.
- Side projects remain prototypes unless their master file says otherwise.
- Intended benefits use `designed to` or equally honest framing when realized adoption or customer impact is unverified.

## Company and recipient claims

Use only details supplied by the user or stated in the JD or profile. Do not convert marketing language into an operational fact. Do not infer a team problem from a job title alone when the inference would carry the message.

Describe overlap precisely:

- Direct overlap: the same meaningful problem or responsibility.
- Adjacent overlap: transferable building blocks or decisions, but a different product or environment.
- Surface overlap: a shared tool or word without a shared problem. Do not build the message around it.

## Human voice

Use `career-tools/reference/humanizer.md` as an internal review reference. Return only the final copy, not its draft and audit sequence.

The output should sound like something Shubh could type or say without rehearsal:

- answer or make the point early;
- use plain words and natural contractions;
- vary sentence length without manufacturing punchlines;
- prefer one concrete detail to a list of adjectives;
- allow honest limits instead of polished overclaiming;
- remove em and en dashes from final candidate-facing copy.

Avoid generic praise, inflated significance, corporate jargon, JD mimicry, forced rule-of-three lists, ceremonial openings, and conclusions that merely restate fit.

## Experience over inventory

Applies to every generated deliverable: application answers, cover letters, outreach, and connection notes.

- Lead with one concrete, relevant thing Shubh actually did, told as a short story with a challenge and outcome, not a list of technologies. Experience that shows facing and overcoming a problem beats a capability inventory.
- Open with the problem or the real-world observation, then the approach, then the outcome. Do NOT open with the technical mechanics of what the system does (what it extracts, retrieves, or calls). Mechanics come after the reader knows why the work mattered. A first sentence that starts with "I built an X that does A, B, C" is the wrong order; recast it to start with the problem that made the work worth doing. This is a recurring failure mode to catch on every draft.
- Do not present a bolded or comma-separated tech-stack list (languages, frameworks, databases) as the case for Shubh. A keyword pile reads like every other applicant and is the opposite of standing out. Name a technology only inside a specific accomplishment where it explains a decision.
- Claim only credentials, skills, and specializations the role actually calls for. An impressive but unrequested credential (a certification the posting never mentions) reads as padding and can actively sink the application. Match the claim to the stated need.
- A specific, genuine, even unusual human story that shows resourcefulness can outperform a prestige signal. This is strongest for startup and founder-direct outreach, where standing out matters most; weight it down for large-company ATS or formal processes where a plain, keyword-legible answer is safer. Never invent the story; use it only when it is real and Shubh can defend it.
- End outreach with the direct ask when there is one ("I'd like to interview for X"), rather than a ceremonial closing.

## Final rejection pass

Reject and rewrite if any answer is yes:

1. Is a candidate or company claim unsupported?
2. Does the evidence need context the reader does not have?
3. Did the draft choose a metric because it looked impressive rather than because it helped?
4. Does it overstate direct overlap, maturity, ownership, scale, or impact?
5. Could the company or recipient name be swapped with little change?
6. Does it delay the answer or intended point?
7. Does it contain generic praise, invented motivation, or AI-sounding filler?
8. Does it lean on a tech-stack keyword list instead of a concrete accomplishment, or claim a credential the role never asked for?
9. Is more than one main angle competing for attention?
10. Would Shubh struggle to explain or defend a sentence in conversation?
11. Does it violate the channel's length, structure, or output contract?

The output is complete only when it passes truth, judgment, standalone clarity, voice, and channel contract checks.
